## Judgment

**Regime logic.** Stage 1 calls a stall-speed expansion with sticky inflation and restrictive real rates, borderline late-cycle, at low confidence (0.36). Taken at face value, expansion would point toward the auto-blend. But two things argue for a late-cycle tilt toward valuation and equilibrium methods. First, the classification is fragile. Second, REITs are long-duration, rate-sensitive equity. Restrictive real rates raise their discount rate and refinancing costs, which a sticky-inflation backdrop makes harder to relieve quickly. The regime_adjusted candidate (11.45%) is built on 180 expansion months, and its forward premium (+7.08%) is already below the unconditional premium (+8.47%), so even the history says expansion is not an especially good starting point for REITs. With 20-30% recession odds, I cap the two backward-looking methods at 15% each rather than letting them carry roughly half the weight, as they do in the auto-blend.

**Valuation context.** The historical candidate (11.94%) leans on a 1990-2026 sample. That sample begins with the post-1990s REIT IPO era at depressed valuations and benefits from a secular fall in rates, which is a tailwind that cannot repeat from today's restrictive real-rate level. Recent realised returns tell a different story: 4.4% over 10 years and 1.4% over 5 years. The cash-flow methods agree closely with each other. Inverse Gordon gives 7.68%, from a 3.75% dividend yield less 2.27% net issuance plus nominal GDP growth. The net-issuance term is real for REITs, which must distribute about 90% of income and fund growth by issuing shares, so I do not add it back. It pairs consistently with aggregate GDP growth, and its WRDS input is rolled forward from 2025-12 rather than stale beyond use. The earnings-yield method (7.42%) understates, because REIT GAAP earnings are net of depreciation, which is why it gets only 10%. There is no long valuation history for REITs, so the Gordon valuation-change term is zero. That is neutral rather than a sign that REITs are cheap, so it does not justify tilting back toward the historical methods.

**Signal alignment.** The composite signal is flat (+0.00), so it neither confirms nor contradicts a move toward the cash-flow and equilibrium methods. The macro alignment signals are near zero. Only financial conditions (+0.11) offer mild support, consistent with easy credit offsetting tight policy. The technical signals conflict. Price sits below its 10-month average (trend -0.26) and the 3-year run looks stretched (mean reversion -0.10), while relative momentum against other asset classes is positive (+0.22). With no directional conviction from the signals, I do not push the estimate toward either end of the range and land between the cash-flow cluster and the historical cluster.

**Rationale.** For REITs the candidates split into two clusters. The backward-looking methods (historical 11.94%, regime-adjusted 11.45%) depend on a favourable 1990-2026 rate tailwind. The forward-looking methods (BL 7.77%, Gordon 7.68%, earnings yield 7.42%) are anchored in current yields and equilibrium. My blend weights the forward-looking cluster at 70%: Gordon 35% for its direct cash-flow basis, BL 25%, and the earnings-yield method 10% because of the depreciation distortion. The historical and regime methods get 15% each, so the long-run premium still counts for something. The result is 8.88%, below the 9.56% auto-blend and inside the [7.42, 11.94] range. It implies an excess return of about 4.85pp over the 4.03% T-bill and a Sharpe ratio of about 0.26 at 18.99% volatility. That sits between the 10-year realised Sharpe (0.20) and the full-history figure (0.40), which I judge plausible for a rate-sensitive real asset under restrictive real rates. Confidence is medium: the dispersion is moderate and the cash-flow methods agree, but the regime call is low-confidence. The governing IPS (config/ips.yaml) is still a draft, so this assumption should be revisited once the policy is finalised.
